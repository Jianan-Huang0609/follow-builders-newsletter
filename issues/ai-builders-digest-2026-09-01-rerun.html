The Bitter Lesson, Reloaded / Bitter Lesson 再解读

强化学习之父直言：今天的大模型训练完就「停止学习」了，这违背了学习最基本的常识。

TD
Training Data
Training Data
中文

Rich Sutton——发明了强化学习、写了这个领域经典教科书、写下《The Bitter Lesson》的人——和他的前学生、Oak Lab 联合创始人 Khurram Javed 一起，指出这个领域有一个盲点：今天的大模型一旦训练完，就不再学习了。

Sutton 把 The Bitter Lesson 浓缩成 26 个字：别被人类知识带偏，把注意力放在能随算力扩展的学习方法上。他对合成数据的态度很直白：「不。这就是个大错误。」Javed 的「大世界假说」支撑了这个观点：世界无限复杂，永远有学不完的东西，从自身经验里学习才是唯一真正能扩展的路径。

最反常识的是他的那个说法：「我没疯，是领域疯了。」他点破了那件人人都习以为常的事——大模型一旦上线，它的「权重就不再改变」——并指出今天真正算得上持续学习的少数例子，是像 Cursor 的 tab 自动补全那样会随使用更新权重的系统。

我没疯，是领域疯了。领域非得管它叫持续学习，它就是学习。

别像 AI 历史上一次次那样被人类知识带偏。把注意力放在能随算力扩展的学习方法上，比如搜索、比如学习。

不。这就是个大错误。……它们的权重从不改变。

English

Rich Sutton — who invented reinforcement learning, wrote the field's seminal textbook, and authored "The Bitter Lesson" — teams up with his former student and Oak Lab cofounder Khurram Javed to argue the field has a blind spot: today's LLMs don't actually keep learning once they're trained.

Sutton distills The Bitter Lesson into 26 words: don't be distracted by human knowledge, focus on learning methods that scale with computation. He's blunt about synthetic data — "No. Just a big mistake." Javed's "big world hypothesis" backs this up: the world is infinitely complex, so there will always be more to learn, and learning from your own experience is the only thing that actually scales.

The contrarian kicker is his framing: "I'm not weird. The field is weird." He points out the obvious thing everyone has normalized — an LLM's "weights never change" once it ships — and notes the few real examples of continual learning today are things like Cursor's tab autocomplete, which actually updates its weights from usage.

I'm not weird. The field is weird. The field, they need to call it continual learning. It's just learning.

Don't be distracted by human knowledge as AI traditionally has been many times. Instead, focus on learning methods that will scale with computation like search and like learning.

No. Just a big mistake. ... Their weights never change.

Personal Agents & the Trust Curve / 个人 Agent 与信任曲线

个人 agent 普及的下一道坎不是能力，是信任。

中文

Peter Yang，做实用 AI 教程和访谈的创作者，抛出一句很容易被忽略、但分量很重的话：「信任将成为个人 agent 普及的最大障碍（也是最大推动力）。」

乍一听像常识，但坐下来想想会发现它很硬核。个人 agent——那种能读你的邮件、花你的钱、帮你排日程的 agent——被采用不是因为它能干，而是因为人们相信它不会搞砸。这份信任，既是今天阻挡普及的墙，也是未来加速普及的引擎。

这条只有 28 个赞，但它比大多数功能路线图之争更精准地点出了真正的瓶颈。

信任将成为个人 agent 普及的最大障碍（也是最大推动力）。

English

Peter Yang, who makes practical AI tutorials and interviews for busy people, drops a one-line thesis that's easy to miss but hard to overstate: "Trust is going to be the biggest barrier (and driver) of personal agent adoption."

It sounds like common sense until you sit with it. Personal agents — the kind that read your email, spend your money, book your meetings — won't be adopted because they're capable; they'll be adopted because people trust them not to screw up. That same trust is both the wall blocking adoption today and the engine that will accelerate it later.

Only 28 likes, but it names the real bottleneck more precisely than most feature-roadmap debates do.

Trust is going to be the biggest barrier (and driver) of personal agent adoption.

English

Dan Shipper, CEO of Every, offers a useful calibration for the AI discourse: anthropomorphizing AI is good when it helps us use AI better — understand it, predict it, work with it — and bad when it's used to sow panic, fear, or unrealistic comparisons to humans.

The Model Frontier Is Now a PM Skill / 模型前沿成为产品经理的必修课

当模型能力变化这么快，理解前沿不再是研究员的专利。

中文

Meta 的 AI 高级总监 Madhu Guru——之前在 Google 带过 Gemini、Veo、Nano Banana——提出一个观点：对 PM 来说，深入理解自己产品所依赖的模型前沿，有「巨大的 alpha」。

他把这拆成一组 PM 应该答得比大多数前沿实验室研究员还好的问题：今天每个规模档的模型各自擅长什么、在哪里会翻车、有什么 workaround 能绕过这些坑、按当前轨迹 2-3 个月后它们大概能做什么——以及你的路线图该怎么跟着调整。

点题的那句是：「这现在就是 PM 工作的核心部分。」不是技术 PM 的加分项，而是入场门槛。

如果你是 PM，深入理解针对你产品和场景的模型前沿，有巨大的 alpha。你要能答得比大多数前沿实验室研究员和 PM 都好：每个规模档的模型今天擅长什么、在哪里翻车、有什么 workaround、按轨迹 2-3 个月后能做什么、路线图该怎么调。

English

Madhu Guru, Senior Director of AI at Meta and previously the person who led Gemini and Veo at Google, argues there's "immense alpha" for PMs who deeply understand the model frontier for their specific product and use cases.

He frames it as a set of questions a PM should be able to answer better than most frontier-lab researchers: what can models in each size class do well today, where does each fail, what workarounds exist, and what will they likely be able to do 2-3 months from now — and how should that reshape your roadmap?

The punchline: this is "a core part of the PM job now." Not a nice-to-have for technical PMs, but table stakes.

If you're a PM, there is immense alpha in understanding the model frontier for your specific product and use cases. You should be able to answer the following better than most frontier lab researchers and PMs for your use cases: 1/ What can models in each size category do well today? 2/ Where does each model fail? 3/ What workarounds can you use to overcome these failures? 4/ Given the trajectory, what will these models likely be able to do 2-3 months from now? How should your roadmap look given this?

English

Box CEO Aaron Levie points to a quiet but significant shift: now that open-weights base models are getting genuinely good and post-training infrastructure is maturing and commercializing, companies with a lot of proprietary data have a real second option — train their own models.

His prediction: the general-purpose frontier models keep a lead in breadth, but we're heading toward a world with "far more models than today across every vertical and domain."
